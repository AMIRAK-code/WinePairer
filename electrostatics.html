1Units and dimensional analysis

Nearly every unit question comes down to rewriting things in the four SI base units that show up here: kg, m, s, A. Memorise these conversions and the rest is algebra:

QuantityUnitIn base units
ForceNkg·m·s⁻²
EnergyJ = N·mkg·m²·s⁻²
ChargeC = A·sA·s
PotentialV = J/Ckg·m²·s⁻³·A⁻¹
Electric fieldV/m = N/Ckg·m·s⁻³·A⁻¹
CapacitanceF = C/Vkg⁻¹·m⁻²·s⁴·A²
ε₀F/m = C²/(N·m²)kg⁻¹·m⁻³·s⁴·A²
k = 1/(4πε₀)N·m²/C²kg·m³·s⁻⁴·A⁻²

Worked example: units of k

[k] = N·m²/C² = (kg·m·s⁻²)(m²) / (A·s)² = kg·m³·s⁻⁴·A⁻²

ε₀ has exactly the inverse units of k (the 4π is dimensionless): kg⁻¹·m⁻³·s⁴·A². A common trap is an option with the right powers but one sign flipped, so check each base unit separately.

Units of things built from E

When a question asks for the units of an expression in E, apply the operator's units:

  • ∇·E or ∇×E: derivatives divide by metres, so V/m² (= ρ/ε₀ for the divergence).
  • Flux ∮E·dA: multiply by m², giving V·m (= N·m²/C).
  • Energy density ½ε₀E²: J/m³. Check: (C²/(N·m²))·(N/C)² = N/m² = J/m³.
  • ∫E·dl: V.

2Fields of standard charge distributions

For symmetric distributions use Gauss's law: pick a surface where E is constant and either parallel or perpendicular to the surface, then

∮ E · dA = Qenclosed / ε₀

Infinite straight wire (charge per length λ)

Use a coaxial cylinder of radius r and length L. Only the curved side has flux: E·(2πrL) = λL/ε₀.

E(r) = λ / (2π ε₀ r) — falls off as 1/r

Infinite plane (charge per area σ)

Use a "pillbox" crossing the plane. Flux exits from both faces: 2EA = σA/ε₀.

E = σ / (2ε₀) — uniform, independent of distance

Two parallel planes, +σ and −σ

Superpose the two fields of σ/(2ε₀). Between the plates they point the same way and add; outside they point opposite ways and cancel.

Boundary conditions across a charged surface

Going through a surface with charge σ:

  • The normal component jumps: E⊥,above − E⊥,below = σ/ε₀ (from a Gauss pillbox).
  • The tangential component is continuous: ΔE∥ = 0 (from ∮E·dl = 0 around a thin rectangle).
Trap: σ/ε₀ is the jump of the normal component. If the question says tangent, the answer is 0.

3Electrostatic potential

The electrostatic field is conservative

The work done by an electrostatic field doesn't depend on the path, so ∮E·dl = 0 and, in differential form,

∇ × E = 0 (the field is irrotational)

That's what lets us write E as the gradient of a scalar: E = −∇V. Compare: "solenoidal" means ∇·B = 0 (true for B, not for E where there's charge, because ∇·E = ρ/ε₀).

From V to E: take the gradient

E = −∇V = −( ∂V/∂x , ∂V/∂y , ∂V/∂z )

Example: V = a x² + b y z gives E = (−2a x, −b z, −b y). Being conservative doesn't make E zero. It only means E is a gradient.

Continuity of V

V is an integral of E. Integrating a function with a finite jump still gives a continuous result with a corner. So:

  • Where E is smooth, V is smooth.
  • Where E jumps (at a surface charge), V stays continuous but has a kink, a point where it isn't differentiable.
  • V would only jump if E were infinite over zero thickness (an idealised dipole layer), which isn't what these questions mean.

From ρ to V: Poisson's equation

Combine ∇·E = ρ/ε₀ with E = −∇V:

∇²V = −ρ/ε₀ (in 1D: d²V/dx² = −ρ(x)/ε₀)

To find V, integrate ρ twice, so each integration raises the polynomial degree by one:

ρ(x)E(x)V(x)

4Electric dipoles

A dipole is +q and −q separated by a distance d. Its dipole moment points from − to +:

p = q d [C·m]

Dipole in a uniform field

Fnet = qE + (−q)E = 0 τ = p × E U = −p · E

Net force appears only in a non-uniform field: F = ∇(p·E). Keep the three quantities apart: force is zero, torque is a cross product (a vector), energy is a dot product (a scalar).

Equivalent charge separation

To give a proton and an electron (charge e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C) the same moment as a molecule, solve p = e·d for d:

d = p / e = 6.1×10⁻³⁰ / 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 3.8×10⁻¹¹ m

That's about a third of the O–H bond length, which tells you water's electrons are only partly shifted toward the oxygen.

5Charges in fields

Equilibrium: electric force balances weight

For a particle at rest, the forces sum to zero: qE = mg, so

|q| = m g / E

Sign: the electric force has to point up. If E points up, q > 0; if E points down, q < 0. Watch your units: mass is often given in grams or mg, and you need kg.

Does a charge follow a field line?

Field lines give the direction of the force (and acceleration), not of the velocity. A particle follows a line only if its velocity stays tangent to it. On a curved line the particle would need a centripetal force pointing toward the curve's centre, but the electric force points along the line. So inertia carries it off the curve.

It follows a field line only when both conditions hold:

  • the line is straight, and
  • the initial velocity is zero or parallel to E.

6Capacitors in series and parallel

Parallel: C = C₁ + C₂ (same V, charges add)
Series: 1/C = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ (same Q, voltages add)
  • Parallel is like making the plate area bigger, so it always increases capacitance.
  • Series is like making the gap wider, so the result is always smaller than the smallest capacitor: C = C₁C₂/(C₁+C₂).
  • Capacitance depends only on geometry and the dielectric. Changing the voltage doesn't change C.

Example with 100 pF and 50 pF: parallel gives 150 pF, series gives 100·50/150 ≈ 33.3 pF.
