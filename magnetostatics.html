1Current, drift velocity and Ohm's law

Where current comes from

A current is charge carriers drifting slowly through the wire. In a time Δt the carriers inside a length vdΔt of wire pass through a cross-section. That's a volume S·vdΔt containing n·S·vdΔt carriers, each with charge q:

I = n q vd S ⇔ vd = I / (n q S) and J = I/S = n q vd

Drift speeds are tiny (about 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻³ m/s) because n is huge (about 10²⁸ to 10²⁹ m⁻³ in metals). The signal travels fast, but the electrons themselves crawl.

Narrowing wire: same current, faster drift

Charge can't pile up anywhere in a steady circuit, so the same I flows through both wires. With I fixed and n the same (both copper), vd ∝ 1/S: a thinner wire means faster drift. It works like water speeding up in a narrower pipe.

Ohm's law, local form

In an ohmic, isotropic conductor the current density at each point is proportional to the electric field at that same point:

J = σ E (σ = conductivity = 1/ρ)

It's E that drives the carriers, not the value of V. Adding a constant to V changes nothing physical, so J can't depend on V itself, only on its slope (E = −∇V). Integrated over a wire, J = σE becomes V = RI with R = ρL/S.

Power dissipated

P = I²R (first find I = n q vd S if you're given the carriers)

2Conductors in equilibrium and RC circuits

A conductor in equilibrium is equipotential

"Equilibrium" means the charges have stopped moving. If there were any field inside, free electrons would move, so E = 0 inside. Then V doesn't change from point to point (ΔV = −∫E·dl = 0), and the whole conductor, surface included, sits at one potential.

What is larger near sharp tips is the surface charge density σ and the field just outside (E = σ/ε₀). The potential isn't. Don't confuse the two.

RC circuit: what happens at t = 0

A capacitor's voltage is VC = Q/C, and charge needs time to flow in. At the instant of connection Q = 0, so VC = 0 and the capacitor behaves like a plain wire. Kirchhoff's loop rule then puts the whole emf across R:

ℰ = VR + VC ⇒ at t = 0: VR = ℰ, I₀ = ℰ/R
later: VC = ℰ(1 − e−t/RC), VR = ℰ e−t/RC

3Magnetic force on a moving charge

F = q v × B |F| = |q| v B sin θ
  • Because of the cross product, F is perpendicular to both v and B, so it's perpendicular to the plane they span.
  • It does no work (F ⟂ v). It bends the path but never changes the speed.
  • It's zero when v ∥ B or v = 0.

Recipe for direction questions

  1. Find the direction of B at the charge's position (for a wire, use the right-hand grip: thumb along I, fingers curl along B).
  2. Compute v × B with the unit-vector rules: x̂×ŷ = ẑ, ŷ×ẑ = x̂, ẑ×x̂ = ŷ, and reversing the order flips the sign.
  3. Flip the result if q < 0.
Shortcut: a positive charge moving away from a wire gets pushed in the direction of the current. One moving toward the wire gets pushed against it. This is the same reason parallel currents attract.

4Magnetic fields of currents

Ampère's law

∮ B · dl = μ₀ Ienc (Ienc = signed sum of the currents through the loop)

Currents in one direction count +, currents in the other direction count −. Currents outside the loop count 0, even though they still create B along it. So the integral is zero when the enclosed currents cancel, for example two equal currents in opposite directions.

Long straight wire (radius a, uniform J)

Take a circle of radius r around the axis, so B·2πr = μ₀Ienc.

  • Outside (r ≥ a): Ienc = I, so B = μ₀I / (2πr).
  • Inside (r < a): Ienc = I·r²/a² (area fraction), so B = μ₀I r / (2πa²), which grows linearly with r.

Centre of a circular loop (Biot–Savart)

Every element dl is at the same distance R from the centre and perpendicular to the radius, so each contributes dB = μ₀I dl/(4πR²), all along the axis. Adding up 2πR of length:

B = μ₀I·2πR / (4πR²) = μ₀ I / (2R)
Trap: μ₀I/(2πR) is the straight wire formula. The loop has no π in it.

Force between parallel wires

Wire 1 makes B₁ = μ₀I₁/(2πd) at wire 2. Wire 2 feels F = I₂LB₁:

F / L = μ₀ I₁ I₂ / (2π d) same direction → attract, opposite → repel

Handy number: μ₀/(2π) = 2×10⁻⁷ T·m/A.

5Electromagnetic induction

Faraday–Lenz

Φ = B A cos θ ℰ = −N dΦ/dt

An emf appears whenever the flux through the circuit changes, whether B changes, the area changes or the angle changes. The minus sign (Lenz's law) means the induced current opposes the change that caused it.

Three ways to change the flux

What changesFluxemf
B grows linearly (dB/dt = k)Φ = N A B(t)ℰ = −N A k (constant)
Coil rotates at ωΦ = B A cos ωtℰ = B A ω sin ωt
B reverses (B → −B)ΔΦ = −2BA per turngives a total charge (below)

Power in a rotating coil

P = ℰ² / R = A² B² ω² sin²ωt / R

Check the powers when choosing an option: ℰ is linear in A and B, so P has A² and B².

Charge from a flux change

The time it takes doesn't matter. Integrate I = ℰ/R over time:

Q = ∫ I dt = (N/R) ∫ |dΦ| = N ΔΦ / R

When B flips from +B to −B, ΔΦ = 2BA, not BA. Forgetting the factor 2 is the classic mistake.
